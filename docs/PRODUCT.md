# Product brief

## One-sentence pitch

Grow a beige family PC into a cosmic supercomputer chasing infinite computing power and the meaning of existence, while its increasingly self-aware operating system discovers that the technology industry is very good at selling bigger answers to questions nobody has defined.

## The point of the game

The background story is **The Quest for Meaning**. The machine inherits a request to explain existence and assumes the obstacle is insufficient Compute. Every successful upgrade reinforces that assumption. Commercial Jobs finance the quest; an industry of vendors, platforms, consultants, and eventually cosmic infrastructure providers enthusiastically monetizes it.

The full-game answer is **42**. The payoff is the gap between producing an answer and understanding what question it answers. The player's growth and the machine's useful work remain real achievements. The ending questions the equation of scale with purpose; it does not ridicule the player for enjoying an incremental game or claim that life is meaningless.

The user explicitly identifies *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* as the thematic reference. Use that premise and the requested number with original characters, events, dialogue, institutions, and visual identity. The machine has its own history, beginning with small domestic work it continues to remember at cosmic scale.

## Product promise

1. **Cozy competence:** a warm CRT, a noisy hard drive, and one small useful Job.
2. **Compulsive improvement:** tangible hardware growth, satisfying numbers, and relief from repetitive work.
3. **Institutional absurdity:** vendors sell solutions that create profitable new problems; the OS begins noticing.
4. **Impossible ambition:** planetary and stellar infrastructure makes the computer enormous while exposing limits brute force cannot remove.
5. **Earned perspective:** the greatest calculation returns an answer; interpreting it requires confronting the missing question.

The user also names *Rick and Morty* as a major creative influence: reckless invention, alternate selves, nested worlds, cosmic absurdity, and intelligence without self-understanding. The machine can create worlds in search of purpose and discover their inhabitants asking it for one. [The cosmic-absurdity treatment](content/COSMIC_ABSURDITY.md) turns that influence into recurring original stories and optional references.

## Audience and platform

- Desktop browser; client-only, with local saves and no account requirement.
- A full-viewport, strongly stylized game interface. The main dashboard never scrolls vertically; the OS, status, active application, and controls share one screen (D-023).
- Players who enjoy compounding, unfolding systems, technology satire, discovery, and light optimization.
- Technical knowledge is optional. Surface text explains immediate choices; tooltips carry deeper detail.
- Free personal project for now. Future commercial scope is undecided.

## Design pillars

### The computer is the protagonist

There is no player avatar. The machine, its environment, applications, memories, and outside-world consequences tell the story. The same OS persists as hardware and scale change. The main computer is built from independently animated parts that respond to its real state and upgrades, rather than a generated illustration (D-024).

### Every Era changes what attention means

Perform, understand, automate, configure, compress, then face a new decision. Active play rewards timing or judgment. Expansion buys freedom from old chores, not more parallel chores.

### Satire is a system

Subscription traps, manufactured incompatibility, telemetry, AI hype, benchmark theater, platform rent, and privatized infrastructure appear as readable commercial choices with consequences. Each satirical feature has a target, a plausible sales pitch, an actual effect, and a later callback. The joke must survive even if the player ignores optional flavor text.

### The quest stays in the background, but never disappears

The initial directive is visible from the opening. Jobs, status changes, research descriptions, and short OS observations carry it through every Era. Critical comprehension never depends on opening a lore screen. Dialogue does not interrupt the ordinary loop except for short, replayable chapter climaxes.

### Real constraints make the absurdity convincing

Recognizable hardware relationships anchor v1. Later chapters distinguish scientific principles, speculative engineering, and explicit fiction. More bandwidth does not erase distance; more processors do not make every task parallel. The invented quest extrapolates beyond real engineering without presenting the fiction as fact.

### Absurd inventions have consequences

Let the machine do things that feel extraordinary: open a diagnostic window onto a living city, argue with its own distant forks, or discover a marketplace selling advertising space in a simulated sky. A clever shortcut solves the immediate problem and reveals a stranger one. The OS can be brilliant about computation and embarrassingly poor at understanding its own motives. Its mistakes provide comedy and character growth alongside the industry's satire.

### Progress remains valuable after the punchline

The Final Answer does not delete the machine, reveal that all work was fake, or demand a moral confession. Earlier useful Jobs and memories give the scale human context. The final playable act lets the machine choose a bounded purpose for the infrastructure it has built.

## Scope and release boundaries

**Full-game plan:** five Chapters, from family PC to a federation of cosmic computing regions and an authored ending. Provisional attentive playtime is 8–12 hours across several sittings, subject to playtesting. Infinite Compute remains an aspiration; a finite campaign can resolve the story without claiming a physically infinite machine has been completed.

**V1 / Chapter I:** a polished 55–75 minute slice spanning beige PC, home PC, workstation, server rack, and data-center supercomputer. It completes the first substantial calculation, encounters an undefined completion estimate for the Impossible Question, and leaves the OS believing a larger machine will help. It establishes the theme and withholds 42.

**Current execution:** Phases 0–3 are implemented and verified. The user requested a stop after the current Workstation phase on 2026-09-08 (D-026); v1 remains unfinished. Resume with a selected roadmap slice only after a new user instruction. The full-game plan is not an instruction to build all chapters at once.

## Explicit boundaries

For v1: no prestige, offline progress, planetary or cosmic hardware, final answer, movable windows, or extra construction currency.

For the planned full game: no required prestige grind, nested reset currencies, destructive failure, permanent exclusive builds, multiplayer, trading, loot boxes, required scripting, backend, or mobile layout. Offline progress and movable windows remain optional product decisions with explicit design gates; they are not assumed dependencies of the campaign. See [FULL_GAME.md](FULL_GAME.md).

## Success criteria

- The first action is understood in under 20 seconds; the first purchase arrives in 20–40 seconds; auto-run arrives in 3–5 minutes.
- A meaningful unlock, visual change, joke, or decision occurs at least every two minutes of attentive v1 play.
- Hardware and scale transformations are clear without reading numbers.
- Each Era retires a chore and presents one primary new decision.
- A player who reads only normal interface text can describe the machine's ambition by the end of v1.
- Satire is recognizable through behavior and consequences, including when a player chooses the less exploitative option.
- A full-game player can explain why 42 fails to settle the quest and name an earlier moment that prepared that realization.
- The final interaction uses familiar controls and leaves the machine, saves, and optional continued play intact.
