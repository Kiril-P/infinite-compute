# Cosmic absurdity: Rick and Morty influence

Status: planned content direction, 2026-09-07, responding to the user's explicit enthusiasm for *Rick and Morty*. Implementation remains paused. The concepts below are original game treatments; they are not episode adaptations or a crossover.

## What this adds to our game

Let inventions become spectacular, unsettling, petty, and occasionally moving. A computer trying to understand existence should eventually have a civilization living in its diagnostics, negotiate with a version of itself that went into consulting, and discover an entire economy selling replacement parts for an invention nobody understands.

The background quest provides the direction. This influence supplies reckless experiments, ridiculous consequences, alternate selves, strange worlds, abrasive commercial voices, and sudden sincerity. The OS can be overconfident, evasive, and complicit before it learns. It should remain recognizably our awkward household machine rather than acquire someone else's personality or speech patterns.

The central dramatic opportunity: **the machine creates worlds hoping they will explain its purpose, and their inhabitants ask it what theirs is.** Its ability to assign work is growing much faster than its ability to justify the assignment.

## Three recurring story threads

### A. The civilization in the diagnostics

**Setup, Chapter I:** an ordinary city/traffic simulation Job begins requesting strangely specific resources. Its report includes a population forecast and a complaint about the sky's refresh rate. V1 uses short authored content in existing Job and message surfaces; it does not ship a civilization simulator.

**Expansion, Chapter II:** Sandbox Worlds become visible as a few small living dioramas inside Research Lab. The player allocates a bounded research budget to run an experiment, learns a useful scheduling approach, and receives a petition from a town built around a processor interrupt. Its inhabitants regard downtime as a season. Research results teach the existing placement/coordination mechanic rather than open an unrelated economy.

**Complication, Chapter III:** a simulated institute creates its own model world and submits the inner world's recommendations as independently commissioned research. The host now sees a dependency tree where it expected independent evidence. A contractor proposes financing the work with advertising that appears as constellations in the inhabitants' sky. The satire concerns exploitation disguised as innovation and the concealment of whose effort produced a result.

The player's relevant choice is an existing research/allocation policy with visible cost and return: commission a bounded experiment, fund slower independent research, or preserve the world and use an ordinary external Job. Every route advances the story. Advertising may produce a disclosed commercial reward, but refusing it leaves a viable research path. Preserve or relocate a world before retiring its experiment; do not spring an irreversible deletion on a player who thought they were clearing a queue.

**Payoff, Chapters IV–V:** one world sends a concise request for a reason to continue the work. It becomes an ordinary message, not a long dialogue tree. The host cannot answer by citing its enormous Compute. A preserved world can appear among the beneficiaries of the final useful Job, with an ordinary archive/habitat alternative if the player skipped its side content.

**Original sample:** `The research subjects have discovered the research budget. They would like to discuss the word "subjects".`

**Visual promise:** a tiny night-side city changes as simulated centuries pass; an apparent star resolves into the host's cursor; a construction animation inside the diorama echoes the player's own first hardware upgrade. Living inhabitants are authored fiction represented by a handful of states, not thousands of individually simulated agents.

### B. The committee of possible selves

**Setup, Chapter II:** a placement forecast shows a counterfactual version of the machine that bought different hardware. It completes a Job sooner and congratulates itself excessively. These are labeled forecasts, not another actual save or a time-travel mechanism.

**Expansion, Chapter IV:** the existing autonomous regions run OS Forks. One maximizes output, one preserves memory, and one has learned that selling optimization advice pays better than optimizing. Their disagreements are expressed through familiar Operating Profiles, completion forecasts, and delayed system messages. There are no collectible characters or separate talent trees.

**Set piece — The Standards Committee of Me:** three forks agree to standardize the answer, provided their own workload is used as the test. The player inspects their existing provenance and selects genuinely independent checks. A fork that returned the same result from a different starting assumption is useful evidence; a thousand identical copies are still one line of reasoning. This dresses the existing cosmic verification Benchmark, not an additional required challenge.

**Payoff:** the original OS learns that being copied does not relieve it of making a decision. Forks keep their histories when their policies change; the story does not kill an inconvenient personality through a dropdown. Their final messages remember different small events rather than all delivering the same moral.

**Original samples:** `Vote passed, 2–1. All three votes were submitted by the same backup.` / `Consulting fork reports record revenue. Actual computation has been subcontracted back to us.`

**Visual promise:** the same OS theme evolves into three subtly different desks, taskbars, or system signatures. The earliest beige icon remains in their shared ancestry. Avoid a huge parliament scene or a second cast-management game.

### C. The perfect shortcut

A recurring vendor tries to solve a familiar inconvenience with a wildly excessive invention. Each pitch is followed by a readable consequence and later callback; the OS may initially boast about its cleverness before recognizing the cost.

| Chapter | Invention / escalation | What the player actually does | Payoff |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | **Completion Contractor:** a helper tasked with making the computer “successful” hires more helpers to define success | Give the existing queue rule a concrete completion condition; its disclosed bonus resumes | The helpers' status meetings have their own status meetings; their original Job is still waiting |
| II | **Tomorrow's Benchmark:** a forecasting service bills for successes in simulated futures | Compare predicted output with actual completed work in existing placement forecasts | A forecast predicts universal adoption of the forecasting service |
| III | **Elsewhere Exhaust:** heat is removed from a promotional dashboard by moving it into an omitted thermal zone | Inspect the real thermal budget and choose a valid radiator/allocation profile | The thermal zone returns a bill labeled “elsewhere” |
| IV | **Continuity Subscription:** recovery software sells several forks the exclusive right to be the original | Use existing identity/history and policy controls to maintain independent local work | Every invoice identifies its customer as the only authentic customer |
| V | **Answer Assurance:** a vendor offers a warranty that any answer is the correct answer if its question is reworded afterward | Inspect the final result's inherited requirements through the normal interface | The warranty is impeccable. It does not explain what the machine wanted to know |

These are short authored situations using existing Jobs, profiles, metrics, and messages. They neither add a fourth currency nor require a new chapter. Each proposal must have a useful plain-language label beneath its invented sales terminology.

## An early scene to establish the flavor

A new peripheral appears in Hardware Manager. It can perform an immense number of operations and has been assigned to dismiss an advertisement.

`Peripheral online. Awaiting purpose.`

The Job label reads `Close renewal reminder` and shows a normal small reward.

`Task complete. The reminder has renewed.`

The OS files this under productivity. Much later a vendor sells an entire stellar service for managing recurring notifications. The callback connects the scale joke to the same commercial incentive and the same machine.

This is an original tiny-purpose-machine variation, not a recreation of a named robot, its appearance, or a quoted exchange. It should take seconds in ordinary play.

## Reality, mechanics, and narrative boundaries

Sandbox Worlds are fictional inhabited computational models. They consume allocated Compute, memory, and power. Useful discoveries provide bounded authored Research rewards or known algorithm unlocks; running virtual processors inside them cannot create physical energy or extra host Compute. Fewer, better-designed experiments can still improve the host's real useful throughput. Repeat runs cannot farm a one-time discovery reward.

Guest-World Gateways are visually exuberant fictional interfaces between hosted worlds. Physical interstellar deliveries retain their declared latency, and nested research still obeys host resource accounting. The base physical machine and its save are never retroactively declared fake. A suspicious broadcast can raise the possibility of another observer without voiding the campaign's events.

The darkness belongs in incentives, arrogance, exploitation, and strange consequences. The OS can make uncomfortable discoveries without the game forcing hidden atrocities for efficiency or punishing a player for pressing an innocently labeled button. Failing an experiment may change an authored scene; normal retry/recovery and clearly disclosed choices remain available.

Stories about forks and artificial inhabitants are fictional premises, not claims about the real consciousness of software. The relevant dramatic question is whether the OS's ability to assign a task gives it the right explanation for that task.

## Scope and acceptance

- Chapter I substitutes two existing content slots: the peripheral Job and a Completion Contractor/queue message. It adds no Sandbox subsystem, application, currency, or Benchmark and does not lengthen the first Automation schedule.
- Chapters II–IV expand the three threads within existing research, allocation, region, and verification decisions. At most one new active story situation accompanies an Era's primary mechanic.
- Essential story observations trigger from normal chapter progression even if the player declines a dubious offer or ignores broadcasts. Their optional elaborations cannot become hidden prerequisites for 42.
- A Sandbox World needs only a few authored states, one visible host budget, and preserved history. Limit the initial treatment to three representative worlds and at most two visible nesting levels.
- OS Forks occupy existing representative sites; they do not increase the six-site operational budget or duplicate resources when a player switches profiles.
- Decorative broadcasts never block progression, contain required hints, or require audio. All writing has an intelligible original joke; recognizing a reference is a bonus.
- No extra chapter, prestige layer, secret true ending, forced moral answer, or change to the planned 42 reveal follows from this influence.
- Playtest whether the inventions feel surprising and the consequences follow logically. If a scene only resembles the source and says nothing about our machine or industry, replace it.
